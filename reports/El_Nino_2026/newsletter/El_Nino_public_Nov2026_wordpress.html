A monthly newsletter from UCSC Ocean Sciences — November 2026 edition

The 2026–27 El Niño has pushed Monterey Bay into record territory. Sea level at Monterey averaged 27 cm (11 in) above normal in October — the highest for any month since the tide gauge was installed in 1973, and above anything in the great El Niños of 1982–83 and 1997–98 — and November is running higher still. The sea surface in the bay is 5.9 °C (11 °F) warmer than normal, the largest departure since our satellite record begins in 1991, and the underwater gliders off Monterey Bay show the upper ocean about 2.3 °C (4.1 °F) warmer than normal, a new high for their twenty-year record. NOAA still gives better-than-even odds that this becomes the strongest El Niño since records began in 1950.

Since 2007, Scripps Institution of Oceanography has kept robotic gliders flying a line that runs from Monterey Bay some 250 miles out to sea. Each glider dives down to 500 m and back up every few hours, measuring temperature, saltiness and oxygen. At UC Santa Cruz we analyse that stream of data each month to track how the 2026–27 El Niño is reaching our coast. Here is where things stand, one month on from our October edition.

What the gliders see

  • A new high for the glider record. The upper ocean off Monterey Bay has been warmer than normal without a break since July 2025. It peaked last April at 1.9 °C above normal, already higher than the peak of the big 2015–16 El Niño (1.8 °C), eased a little over the summer, and has climbed since September to about 2.3 °C in late October — a new record for the 20-year glider record (the previous high, 2.2 °C, came in late 2017) — and has stayed there into mid-November. On the most recent full crossing of the line (20 October–4 November), the top 100 m averaged 1.8 °C above normal, up from 1.2 °C on the previous crossing, and the water from 100 m down to 500 m 0.5 °C — the warmth is pushing the ocean's density layers 27–44 m deeper than usual.
  • The warmest sea surface in the satellite record. The season of upwelling — the wind-driven welling-up of cold water that the gliders map along the whole coast — is over, and with it the supply of cold water that kept a lid on the heat through the summer. The marine heatwave that flared in the bay on 2 October has not let up, and it is now rated "severe", a level the bay had not reached on any day since our satellite record begins in 1991. In the week ending 16 November the sea surface in Monterey Bay averaged +5.9 °C above normal (it was +3.4 °C a month ago), with +3.9 °C just outside the bay and +3.8 °C along the whole 250-mile glider line; a NOAA weather buoy in the bay, an independent thermometer, averaged 19.6 °C (67 °F) over the same week. On 12 November the bay's surface reached about 20 °C (68 °F), 6.2 °C above normal — warmer than on any day, in any season, since 1991; the previous high was 18.2 °C, in August 1997. (The latest satellite values are preliminary.) The glider logged its own warmest reading of the year, 6.1 °C above normal at 10 m depth, on 2 November. Monterey Bay has now spent 201 of the 565 days since May 2025 in marine-heatwave conditions. NOAA's heatwave tracker now ties the two together: the heatwave off California began before the El Niño, but "we are seeing additional warming due to El Niño, primarily through the arrival of Kelvin waves".
  • A record month for sea level — and no retreat. The coastal Kelvin wave that arrived in early October — a pulse of raised sea level travelling up the coast from the tropics — did not pass through and leave (Figure 2). Sea level at Monterey averaged 27 cm (11 in) above normal over October as a whole: the highest monthly mean since the gauge began in December 1973, 5 cm above the previous record (November 1982) and above every month of the 1982–83 and 1997–98 El Niños. At San Francisco, where measurements go back to 1897, October brought the highest monthly mean water level in 129 years of records. (That one is helped by the long-term rise of the sea itself, about 2 mm a year at that gauge; measured against that rising baseline, October ranks fourth, behind months in early 1983 and 1998.) And the water has stayed up: since the start of October the daily sea level at Monterey has not dropped below 18 cm above normal, the first 17 days of November averaged 32 cm (13 in), and 13 November brought a one-day spike to 45 cm (18 in) at Monterey and 52 cm (20 in) at San Francisco. The November figures are preliminary, and weather adds to or subtracts from any single day. In 1982–83 and 1997–98 the highest sea levels came between November and March, so the season is still young.
  • The El Niño itself keeps strengthening. NOAA's El Niño index now stands at +2.5 °C, up from +2.2 °C a month ago — the highest on record for this time of year, already above the peaks of the 1982–83 and 1997–98 El Niños, and exceeded only in the winter of 2015–16 (+2.6 °C). In its 12 November update NOAA reports the key tropical Pacific region at +2.5 °C, expects the El Niño to keep strengthening before it begins to weaken in early 2027, and puts the chance of a strong-to-very-strong event lasting through March 2027 at more than 79%. Its odds that this becomes the strongest El Niño since records began in 1950 are now about 60% — down from 83% a month ago, but still better than even.

What this could mean for the Monterey Bay region

  • Higher water at the coast — it is here. Strong El Niños raise California sea level by up to about a foot for months at a time; this one has done so since early October, and Monterey has just logged its highest month on record. Stacked on winter storms and high tides (the season's king tides fall on 24–26 November, 23–25 December and 21–22 January), that means more coastal flooding and beach and bluff erosion; Scripps researchers expect the highest coastal sea levels ever recorded in California this winter. It has already begun: NOAA reports that in late October four Southern California tide gauges recorded their highest water levels ever, with Monterey reaching its seventh highest, and in mid-November large swells prompted an evacuation warning for part of the coast near Capitola.
  • Changes in what lives here. The past year's heatwave has already brought seabird die-offs, warm-water species farther north than usual, renewed stress on kelp forests, and — this June — two humpback whales found dead on Monterey Bay beaches with the algal toxin domoic acid in their systems. This autumn MBARI measured record warmth at its mooring in the bay and found juvenile pelagic red crabs, a warm-water species, farther north than it had ever seen them, and California has delayed the commercial Dungeness crab season until at least 1 January because of humpback whale entanglements. A strong El Niño tends to prolong those conditions. The last big marine heatwave shows how costly that can be locally: during the 2014–16 "Blob" a toxic algal bloom put enough domoic acid into Dungeness crab that California delayed the opening of the 2015–16 season until late March 2016, and entanglements of whales in fishing gear — about ten a year before 2014 — jumped to 53 in 2015 and 55 in 2016 as the warm water squeezed whales and the fish they eat into a narrow band near shore (UC Santa Cruz research; NOAA Fisheries).
  • A leaner ocean. El Niño weakens upwelling, and with it the nutrients that feed the plankton at the base of the food web that supports our salmon, anchovy, seabirds and whales. NOAA's ecosystem assessments flag reduced productivity as the main ecological risk for 2026–27.
  • Less oxygen at depth. The gliders also measure dissolved oxygen. Water arriving along our coast now carries roughly 30 µmol/kg (14%) less oxygen than usual for its density, a little less than last month — a sign that warm, oxygen-poor water from the south is being carried northward along the coast. That matters for fish and invertebrates that live near the seafloor.

What we are watching next month

Whether November's sea level tops October's record, and how high the December king tides and the first big winter storms push the water on top of it; whether the heat at the surface of the bay survives those storms; whether the upper ocean keeps warming past its new record as the glider makes its next full crossing of the line; and what NOAA's December update says about the El Niño's peak.
